**PANDO**

**Pando Strategy — Current Direction**

*August 2026\. Written to be read once and explained back. Detail lives in the appendices.*

**1\. What Pando is**

**One sentence:** Pando is a text line where a parent can ask anything she would ask a trusted parent group chat — including things she would never ask under her own name — and get answers based on the real, recent experience of nearby parents.

A parent saves one phone number and texts it like a person. "Is there a good music class for an active 18-month-old near Pasadena?" "Does anyone know a patient swim teacher?" "Is $28 an hour normal for a nanny here?" The problem is that the best answers to these questions already exist — in other parents' heads — but there is no good way to reach them. Google returns articles written for search engines. ChatGPT knows facts about the world but doesn't know that three families two streets away tried that exact class in June. Reddit and Facebook groups require posting under a name, in public, and hoping the right person happens to scroll past. Review sites average away everything that matters. WhatsApp groups hold real knowledge and then lose it — right group, right moment, then it scrolls away forever. And the most important questions never get asked anywhere, because every option requires showing your face. Pando fixes both halves: it remembers what local parents have learned, and it lets anyone ask without social risk. Activities, classes, camps and caregivers are the areas Pando is deliberately strongest in first. They are a starting point, not the boundary — a parent can ask anything.

**2\. What using Pando should feel like**

**An ordinary question.** "What's a really good music class for an active 18-month-old near Pasadena?" Pando checks what it already knows. If nearby parents have shared real experience, the answer comes back in about a minute: "Three parents near you have used Toddler Tunes in the last six months — all with high-energy kids around that age. Two said the 9am class is calmer than the 10:30. It runs about $30 a session. One tip: it fills fast after Labor Day. Want me to check current openings?" Every part of the answer says where it came from and how recent it is. If Pando doesn't know enough, it says so honestly — and offers to ask around.

**A caregiver question.** "Does anyone know a nanny who might be available three afternoons a week from September?" This is where Pando can do something nobody else can. Parents have privately told Pando about caregivers they've personally employed — including, sometimes, that their own nanny will have free hours soon because a child is starting school. None of that is posted anywhere public. Pando replies: "There may be someone. A family nearby says their nanny of three years will have afternoons free from September. If you'd like, I can check whether she's interested — she decides whether to be introduced." Nobody's name or number moves until both sides say yes.

**A sensitive question.** "Does anyone else secretly hate being home with their toddler all day?" The first reply is human, not clinical: "You're not the only one — thank you for trusting Pando with this." Then Pando shares what other local mothers have said about exactly this, anonymously and with their permission. If it would genuinely help, Pando quietly asks two or three well-matched mothers who have offered to answer questions like this — showing the asker first exactly how her situation will be described, and letting her change it. She can read what parents said, ask one anonymous follow-up, and — later, if both people want it — Pando may introduce two mothers to each other. Nobody ever knows who asked. This costs nothing. A struggling parent should never have to reach for a credit card at the moment she admits she's struggling.

(One note for the future, not for now: as Pando learns a family deeply, a natural later product is Pando as concierge — "just handle the camp forms." Parked deliberately in the later file.)

**3\. Where Pando gets its answers**

An answer can contain up to four kinds of material, and Pando always says which is which: **general information** (clearly labeled as such — never dressed up as a parent's opinion); **experience parents have already shared** (with when, and how old their child was); **fresh private answers**, when Pando personally asks a few relevant parents right now; and **the open board** — your question, anonymous, in a short daily digest that local parents browse and answer if they actually know (slower and broader than a private ask). Separately, a person at Pando checks anything sensitive or high-stakes before it's used — that's a safety layer, not a source: Pando staff never write the answers. Pando never lets AI-generated text or public information pose as a real parent's recommendation — the labels ("3 parents · confirmed 4 weeks ago") come from records, not from the AI's imagination. Freshness matters because family life moves: teachers change, prices change, the great class from 2023 has a different instructor now. Old knowledge is marked as old, and Pando quietly re-confirms things with the parents who originally shared them.

**4\. What Pando learns about parents**

Pando privately learns things like: children's ages, neighborhood, school or preschool, community groups, how long the family has lived locally, the work-and-childcare setup, how much local support exists, practical constraints, preferred times, willingness to drive, spending preferences, and topics where the parent has real lived experience. The purpose is one question: **who is likely to have experience genuinely useful to this parent?** A recommendation for a Saturday class means more coming from a working mother with a same-age child three blocks away than from a stranger across the city. The principle: **Pando should know enough about you to find the right people, without exposing you to them.** What Pando knows privately is much more specific than anything it ever reveals — the asker appears to others only as, say, "a Pasadena parent with an 18-month-old and limited backup care."

**5\. What Pando learns from every recommendation**

"Little Maestros is great" is not knowledge — it's a mood. Pando asks a little more: Did you use it yourself? When? How old was your child? What was particularly good? What were the drawbacks? Who is it especially right for — and who might it not suit? Roughly what did it cost, and was it worth it? Would you recommend it again? Would you answer a follow-up from another parent? That extra minute is what makes the answer reusable: the next parent doesn't get a star rating from strangers, she gets "recommended by three parents with toddlers, one confirmed last month, best for high-energy kids, skip it if yours is noise-sensitive." Over time, this should make Pando more useful than any review site — because reviews tell you what an anonymous crowd thought, and Pando tells you what someone like you actually experienced.

**6\. How Pando chooses which parents to ask**

Pando never sends a question to everyone — that's how group chats train people to ignore things. It chooses a few parents for whom the question is genuinely theirs to answer: a recent same-stage child, nearby, ideally a shared school or community, similar practical constraints, and a track record of answers other parents found useful. Being asked should feel like a compliment — "you specifically, because your kid did this" — which is why response rates to a personal request are several times higher than to a broadcast. Pando also protects its helpers, deliberately and mechanically: at least five days between requests to the same person; anyone who actually answered in the last week drops down the list even if she has budget left (answering is the effort, not being asked); rotation among equally suitable people rather than leaning on the same generous few; and never exceeding anyone's monthly limit. Every request carries an effortless exit — reply PASS and the question moves to someone else immediately, with no follow-up, no penalty, and nothing recorded against you. A parent who passes often is telling us our matching is wrong, not that she's unhelpful. The best people, not the most people.

**7\. Community participation — the five-question agreement**

Pando's private knowledge comes from parents helping each other, so free access to it comes with one commitment: **you agree Pando may ask you up to five genuinely relevant community questions a month.** (This supersedes the earlier three-question rule.) Five is a ceiling, not a schedule — most months will be fewer, many will be zero, because Pando only asks when your experience is truly relevant. You can skip any individual request, no questions asked. Your own questions never count against it; neither does anything you volunteer yourself, nor replies in a conversation you already agreed to. Every kind of proactive request draws from this one budget — fresh-answer requests, sensitive-community questions, caregiver questions, "is this still current?" checks — so there is exactly one number to understand. The choice on screen is written like a person talking: "**Now and then**" (up to 5 a month) · "**Happy to help more**" (up to 10\) · "**Ask me anytime it's genuinely relevant.**" Whatever the setting, Pando spaces requests at least five days apart — so "five a month" describes a real ceiling rather than a burst. Two things don't touch this budget at all: answering questions you find yourself on the open board, and replying to items in your Digest — you chose to look, nobody summoned you. But they absolutely **count as giving**: answer board questions regularly and you've earned community access exactly as if Pando had asked you. A parent who doesn't want any commitment can still use Pando and buy anything on it — at full price. Participation is what earns free access to the community's knowledge and member pricing. That's not a punishment; it's how a village works: everyone who draws water helps carry some, or pays the water-carrier. **Not yet decided:** when the same few highly relevant parents are needed by both a free sensitive question and a paid request in the same week, which goes first. We have not resolved this and it needs a rule before launch.

**8\. Network Asks**

A **Network Ask** (the current name for what we earlier called a Network Check) is what happens when Pando doesn't already know enough and goes to get fresh information from the right parents. The versions, simply: **Passive** — free; your question is saved, and you hear the moment the network can answer it well. **Board Ask ($5)** — your question (anonymous) is posted to a once-a-day digest that opted-in parents browse; anyone who knows the answer chimes in; if the board hasn't cracked it in two days, Pando taps one well-matched parent so you're never left hanging. **Targeted Ask ($15)** — Pando personally asks three to five carefully matched parents; questions with unusual or stacked requirements automatically get a human reviewing the match (this absorbed what we once called Precision). **Last-Minute Care** — urgent same-day sitter help; offered **free during the pilot**, honestly labeled ("free while we're in pilot — and we'll always tell you straight if we can't find someone"), in the strongest neighborhoods only. It's the single best advertisement for membership we have. Every paid Ask is guaranteed: no useful answer inside the promised window, automatic credit — and that guarantee is the point of the price. A parent isn't buying advice, which she rightly expects to be free among neighbours; she's hiring Pando to go and do the work, and to keep working until she has something useful or she pays nothing. Prices are deliberately gentle because Asks are the front porch, not the business: **the goal is membership.** At $19 a month, two questions pay for it — the arithmetic sells itself. The commercial principle: knowledge the community already shared is free through participation; parents pay when they ask Pando to actively do more work. The first Targeted Ask is free, because the pilot's most important paid question is simple: after experiencing one, does she pay for the second?

**9\. Sensitive questions — community when you don't already have one**

Sometimes the parent isn't really asking "what information do I need?" She's asking **"am I the only one?"** Loneliness. Going back to work. Who you are after becoming a mother. Ordinary marriage strain. Not enjoying a stage everyone says to treasure. For a mother with close mom friends, these questions have a home. For one without — new to town, first baby, working odd hours — there is nowhere, and the alternative to Pando isn't a competitor, it's silence. Pando offers **community on demand**: immediate warmth, real experiences from local mothers who've been there, and — where it helps — a small, carefully matched private ask. She sees exactly how Pando will describe her before anyone is contacted, and approves or edits it. She gets a summary, can choose "see what parents said" for the individual perspectives (only where each contributor permitted it), can ask one anonymous follow-up, and later, if both people want it, Pando can introduce two mothers directly. All of this is free, permanently. The helpers come from a quiet, warm opt-in during signup — "Some parents ask Pando the things they can't ask anywhere else. Would you be open to occasionally answering one, anonymously?" — **"I'll be a listening ear" / "Not for me right now."** It spends the same monthly allowance as everything else; the flag just tells Pando which of those asks may be sensitive ones. And permission for a follow-up is asked at the natural moment — right after a helper writes her answer ("Can the asker send one anonymous follow-up?") — never buried in signup. Separately and non-negotiably: medical emergencies, serious health or legal issues, abuse, self-harm, and child-safety situations get professional information and human escalation — never crowd opinion. Anything naming a specific person negatively goes to human review and is never published.

**10\. The Pando Digest — useful before you ask**

Pando shouldn't only answer questions; it knows enough about a family to occasionally say something useful before they think to ask. The Digest is a short, personal text — never a newsletter — that should read like a well-connected friend: **"A few things worth knowing for Jasper this week: ① Registration just opened for two toddler programs parents near you recommend. ② Parents with kids his age are asking about dropping to one nap. ③ You wanted weekend options — a new Saturday class opened nearby. Reply 1, 2 or 3 for details."** Its ingredients: age and stage, local timing (camp sign-ups, class openings), what similar parents are asking, fresh confirmations from real parents, caregiver availability (with consent), upcoming needs, and gentle loop-closing ("You asked about music classes — did you try one?" — answers that make Pando smarter). The hard rule: **if Pando doesn't have something unusually relevant for this family right now, it sends nothing.** Silence is a feature; the Digest earns trust by being scarce. Our recommendation on frequency: event- and stage-triggered with a ceiling of one every two weeks, SMS-only in the pilot, hand-curated at first. Access works in layers: the Digest text itself — the headlines — goes to everyone free; it's the advertisement. Clicking into the depth (the actual records, "see what parents said," details, booking help) is for community members. And there's one more door in: **answer one open-board question, and the week's Digest opens fully for you** — reciprocity you can feel instead of read about. Pando+ later adds the richer layer on top — saved interests, earlier alerts, automatic follow-ups, caregiver intelligence, deadline tracking. Strategically the Digest is **primarily a retention engine** — the thing that makes Pando a weekly presence between questions — with growth as a side effect (a genuinely useful item gets forwarded). It's likely the strongest habit mechanism we have. The smallest testable version is in Appendix D.

**11\. Caregivers — six real needs**

Care is where families spend the most money on the least information. Pando organizes it around what parents actually need: **Night nurse / newborn care** — rare, expensive, found by whisper; Pando's consented network can surface names with real employment history behind them. **Full-time nanny** — the highest-stakes hire most families ever make; Pando can search its network and check references from parents who actually employed her. **Part-time nanny** — the hardest shape to find (everyone wants the same afternoons); Pando's advantage is knowing when good caregivers' hours free up. **Nanny share** — two families \+ one caregiver; Pando is unusually placed to match the families (stage, neighborhood, schedule) as well as the caregiver. **Babysitter, planned** — date-night depth from sitters other local parents actually use. **Babysitter, same-day** — the hardest and most valuable; deliberately last, once supply is proven. In every case: discovering what the network knows is free; parents pay when Pando does real matching work — and the price follows the work. A match from the network (Pando finds the person, confirms interest and fit, and introduces) is light: a planned sitter $49, part-time nanny or share $199–249, full-time nanny $299. A full search — where Pando actively looks, verifies references, and stands behind the result — is a different product at a different price: around $699, or $449 for Pando+ annual members. Members pay less across the board (a sitter match $29, a nanny match $229), which is deliberate: one nanny hire at member pricing pays for a year of Pando+ several times over. A replacement guarantee on full searches is right eventually, and dishonest until we have enough supply to actually replace someone — so it arrives with density, not with launch. Every caregiver becomes visible only by her own consent, step by step — a parent tells Pando privately, may invite her, and nothing is shown to anyone until she claims her profile and chooses what to share (roles, hours, rates, and when she might become available; past employers can act as references). Quality has a hard floor: **a nanny is listed only with two firsthand references, one being the family who added her** — one reference means she's held, with an explanation, for human review. (Babysitters: one reference plus identity verification.) Availability starts simple: no calendar in the pilot — instead a text opt-in ("Want us to text when a family nearby needs someone? Reply with the days you're usually free"), with real calendars arriving alongside booking later. A caregiver can also privately opt in to hear about work below her stated minimum rate — visible only to her and Pando, so a "no" can quietly become "maybe, at the right price." This is why Pando may know about trusted caregivers before they appear anywhere public — the single most valuable piece of information in family life, volunteered nowhere else. **None of this is charged for until our California legal review clears the structure**, and one open pricing idea (a small per-alert charge for caregiver-availability hits) sits with counsel explicitly. Full pricing in Appendix A.

**12\. Keeping caregiver relationships connected to Pando**

Once Pando introduces a family and a caregiver, they could simply swap numbers and vanish — and trying to trap them would poison everything. The right question is: what can Pando keep doing that both sides genuinely want? For the family: references done properly, background checks, interview scheduling, and — later — payments, payroll and tax administration (which most families handle badly and hate). For the caregiver: being findable by good families without an agency taking 20% of her wage, references that travel with her, and a place to say "my hours free up in June." **In the pilot, introductions are free and manual** — the fee was always waiting on legal review anyway, and free-with-full-choreography tests the process while making every good match an advertisement. How it works in the pilot, concretely: both sides talk only to Pando. Pando texts the caregiver: "A family near Madison Heights is looking for Tuesday and Thursday afternoons — interested?" Questions relay both ways, first names and context only. When **both** say "yes, connect us," Pando sends each a contact card — and checks in with both sides two weeks later. Later, the built version: each match gets a temporary Pando relay number; they text it like a normal number and it forwards, real numbers invisible; when both elect to connect directly, numbers are exchanged, the relay retires — and the family's match fee (post-legal-clearance) lands at that same moment, framed always as paying for the completed matching work — references checked, fit confirmed, both sides interested — never for "unlocking a phone number." The relay number quietly doubles as the rebooking channel ("text the same number to book her again"), which keeps the relationship on Pando by being useful, not by trapping anyone. Pando never reads or polices the messages. Detail in Appendix B.

**13\. Founding Contributors and active members**

Founding Contributors are the parents who build the first knowledge base before launch — invited personally, asked for a profile and two real firsthand recommendations, each read by a human before it's added. Approval matters more than submission because the knowledge base is only worth what's actually in it. During the seed phase they're thanked with $15 per added contribution and a prize-draw entry — a thank-you for founding labor, not a permanent wage; over time, cash gives way to something better: **helping Pando makes Pando better for you.** And helpers should know they're valued, not deduce it. Every contributor has a private ledger — her **grove**. Each answer that's added and helps a family grows it by one **leaf**, and leaves convert at posted, knowable rates: **5 leaves \= a free Targeted Ask · 15 \= a free month of Pando+ · 40 \= a free year.** Around it: impact receipts ("your answer reached her in 20 minutes — she booked it"), a quarterly personal note ("your grove: 23 answers, 31 families helped"), and earned statuses with real perks — Founding Parent, Trusted Contributor, Local Expert. Occasionally something small and real arrives unannounced — a coffee on Pando at the place three of her neighbours recommended, early access to camp sign-ups. That stays a surprise rather than a posted rate: the moment generosity has an advertised price, it stops being generosity and starts being piecework. What nobody ever sees: anyone else's leaves. Progress you can feel, milestones you can reach, generosity made visible to you — with the competitive surface permanently absent. No public scores, no leaderboards. Referrals: invite another parent, and when she completes her profile and her first contribution is added, you earn a free Targeted Ask (up to three). Details in Appendix C.

**14\. Schools and community partnerships**

For the Pasadena pilot, Pando will run **free partnerships with a small number of schools and preschools** — not to sell them anything, but because a school is the natural front door to exactly the parents who most need Pando (new families, incoming classes). The model is simple: the school shares one invitation; parents join through a school-specific link; the affiliation is privately confirmed and used only to match better. The boundaries are absolute: the school never sees individual questions, never receives parent data, never moderates anything — and Pando never becomes an anonymous complaint board about the school. The link mechanics already exist: a school-specific invite code (e.g. poly-fall26) automatically and privately tags everyone who joins through it. That affiliation then quietly improves matching: same-school parents rank higher for school-adjacent questions, incoming-class parents get matched to the stage-mates who matter most in September, and school-calendar items can appear in their Digests. One more idea we're adopting in reframed form: schools may volunteer **two staff members a semester as "School Facts" contributors** — their answers enter the knowledge base clearly labeled "from \[School\] staff — official information," and Pando combines them with parent experience: "The school says aftercare runs to 6pm ($12/day). Two parents add: Tuesdays get crowded — book by Sunday." Facts from the institution, texture from parents, labels telling them apart. Staff are a labeled source, never a gatekeeper — nothing waits on them, and parent questions never route to the school first. What the school gets: a genuinely useful resource for its families, new-family integration without another coffee morning, somewhere to send the "does anyone know a good…" questions staff can't safely answer, and equal access to community knowledge for families who don't yet know anyone. Later, this can become paid — school-sponsored Pando for all families — and of the longer-term partnership ideas, two deserve a permanent place in the strategy: **schools/preschools** and **employers (as a return-to-work family benefit)**. The rest — relocation services, health systems, residential communities — are distractions for now. Detail in Appendix E.

**15\. How Pando makes money**

The sequence, simply: parents pay for **Network Asks** when they want fresh, specific work done (first one free). Then **caregiver matching** — the highest-value moments in family life — once legal review clears it, followed over time by the services around care: references, background checks, and eventually payments and payroll. Then **Pando+ (\~$19/month · $149/year)**, the recurring engine — included Asks, the richer Digest, priority handling, and the caregiver intelligence layer: availability alerts, earlier access to newly available caregivers, priority in matching. Caregiver matching itself stays separately priced for everyone — members simply pay much less (a sitter match $49 / $29 member; a nanny match $299 / $229; a full search $699 / $449 for annual members) — because folding high-labour matching into a $19 subscription would sink it, and because the paid match must stay legally distinct. The membership decision should therefore make itself at the moment of greatest need: a family facing a nanny search saves more than a year of Pando+ by joining first, which is exactly the behaviour we want. Alongside: **school and later employer partnerships** that pay for their families' access. Eventually, possibly, a **parent-to-parent marketplace**. Three permanent rules: no sponsored answers, no pay-to-play recommendations, and no commercial relationship may ever influence what Pando says is good. Full ranking in the Revenue Strategy; every number in Appendix C.

**16\. The future parent marketplace**

Families are constantly buying, selling, and giving away the equipment of childhood — and Pando will know things no marketplace knows: children's exact stages, what each family is aging out of and into, location, communities, and trust. "Pando, I want to sell our travel cot" could someday route directly to a nearby family expecting their second — no photographing, listing, or haggling with strangers. Different from Facebook Marketplace because it's matched (stage-aware), local, and between parents who share a trust network. It is explicitly **not** for the pilot — it earns a place only after the core habit exists. Sketch in Appendix F.

**17\. What the pilot actually needs to prove**

Seven numbers, not thirty: **(1)** Do parents ask a second question, unprompted, in weeks 3–6? **(2)** Do they use Pando across more than one kind of need? **(3)** After the free premium experience, do they pay for another — specifically, do at least eight different households pay a second time with real money? **(4)** Do contributors still respond by their third request, and does answering stay spread across many people rather than a heroic few? **(5)** Are answers actually useful — do parents say "this helped me decide"? **(6)** Do sensitive questions appear, and do askers say they felt less alone? **(7)** Does Pando increasingly answer from what it already knows instead of asking the network again — the sign the knowledge is compounding? Rules we've committed to in advance: results are counted without anything the founder personally rescued; too little data means "extend and narrow," never "declare victory"; a serious safety failure overrides every good number. Green across the board → expand and build Pando+. Mixed → narrow to the strongest neighborhoods and question types and re-read in a month. One branch is pre-committed rather than improvised: if parents clearly use and value Pando but won't pay per question — strong unprompted asking, high usefulness, weak second purchases — Asks become free, rationed by rules instead of price (a few free Asks a month for everyone, more plus priority for members), and monetisation concentrates on membership and caregiver work. That is a planned response to one specific pattern, not a fallback for disappointing results generally; if usage and payment both fail, the answer is to narrow or rethink, not to make failure free. Demand or usefulness clearly failing on healthy data → stop or rethink, honestly.

**18\. What comes next**

**Seed (now):** \~350 personal invitations; build the first real knowledge base; everything reviewed by hand. **Pasadena pilot (October):** real questions, real matching, real payments, the seven numbers. **After the pilot:** deepen what parents actually used; drop what they didn't. **Then:** Pando+ and the richer Digest. **Alongside:** caregiver products wherever real demand meets consented supply and legal clearance. **Next:** a second community, led by a paid, disclosed local connector — the test being whether the playbook works without the founder in the room. **Later:** caregiver infrastructure (payments, payroll), the marketplace, and selective school/employer partnerships. **Not building yet:** an app, same-day care, placement fees, the marketplace, employer deals, or any second market before the first one passes its own test.

**19\. How Pando stays trustworthy without**

A human reads everything that matters. That is the promise, and it is also the obvious way to strangle the company — so the design principle is: software prepares, humans decide. Pando's tools draft the candidate matches, extract the structured details from a parent's message, strip identifying information, flag what looks thin, and write a short reason for concern — leaving a person a few seconds to approve, adjust, or ask one follow-up question. The review queue already works this way. References follow the same logic: a structured text survey to the families who employed a caregiver does the routine work, and a real conversation is reserved for full-time placements, where the stakes justify it. As the knowledge base grows, review becomes proportionate rather than uniform: ordinary recommendations from contributors with a track record can pass automatically, while anything sensitive, anything about a named person, and everything caregiver-related keeps human eyes permanently. Not in the pilot, though — for the first months every contribution is read by a person, because that is how we learn what "good" looks like well enough to safely automate any of it.

Only the genuine ones: **(1)** the priority rule when free sensitive questions and paid Asks need the same scarce contributors; **(2)** exact Pando+ price and included-Ask count (decided from pilot data); **(3)** the contest prize and its legal rules; **(4)** whether seed contributors can gift their $15 forward; **(5)** final community-list edits; **(6)** QuitCode's committed Phase 1 date — which sets the seed launch date; **(7)** minimum sample sizes before showing local pay benchmarks; **(8)** the trademark classification outcome (two applications filed as instructed); **(9)** whether a small per-alert charge for caregiver-availability notifications is legally viable (with counsel).
